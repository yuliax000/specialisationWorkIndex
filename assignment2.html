Sound Characterization Experiments

My first area of exploration focuses on the sound characterization of the instrument. In my first assignment, I proposed creating a musical experience without negative feedback, where toddlers can freely experiment without producing obviously “wrong” results. While controlling random pitches can help maintain harmony, the timbre and behavior of the sounds are also important in shaping the overall experience.

I am particularly concerned with playfulness, sustainability and harmony. The sounds should be enjoyable enough to encourage repeated interaction, while remaining pleasant during longer periods of play. As users can continuously generate and layer sounds, I also want to explore how different sound characteristics influence the resulting harmony and overall experience.

For this experiment, I developed four prototypes exploring different timbres, note lengths, effects and harmonic structures. These include a simple dry sound, a keyboard-style timbre, a sound with longer envelope release and reverb, and a piano-based chord system. By comparing these approaches, I aim to understand which sound character best supports repeated exploration while maintaining a playful and harmonious experience.

Prototype 1: Simple Dry Sound

The first prototype uses a simple dry sound, with a sine oscillator as the basic timbre and no reverb. A sine wave has a simple sound structure without complex harmonics, providing relatively clean and direct auditory feedback. I chose it as the first prototype to establish a baseline for comparison with later versions using different timbres, effects and harmonic structures.

Prototype 2: keyboard-like timbre

The second prototype uses an oscillator and envelope to simulate a keyboard-like timbre. Compared with the simple and pure sine wave used in the first prototype, this sound has a richer character and is closer to the sound of a traditional instrument. I included this prototype to test whether changing the timbre alone, without significantly changing the sound-generation logic, could make the instrument more interesting and playful, and encourage children to interact with it repeatedly.

Prototype 3: reverb and long release

The third prototype uses a longer envelope release and reverb, allowing each note to last longer and naturally overlap with subsequent notes. I designed this version to test whether the extended and spatial sound could create a more comfortable playing experience, better connect the sound with the weather theme, and allow continuous interaction to gradually build a more complete and immersive musical soundscape. It also tests whether the sound can remain clear and pleasant when multiple notes overlap.

Prototype 4: keyboard-like chords

The fourth prototype uses piano-based chords. Instead of generating a single random note for each interaction, it directly triggers chords made up of multiple notes. I included this version to explore whether pre-built harmony could provide richer and more pleasant musical feedback. I also wanted to compare system-generated harmony with harmony created by gradually layering individual random notes, and explore which approach better supports the project's focus on exploration and open-ended play.

Mapping Prototypes

In the second experiment, I want to explore how user actions are mapped to auditory feedback, and whether users expect to have more direct control over the sound. Triggering music and percussion at different stages of the interaction may create different cause-and-effect relationships and affect the overall playing experience.

In particular, I want to test whether delayed feedback is intuitive and easy to understand. Immediate auditory feedback is generally more intuitive, as users can quickly recognize the connection between their actions and the resulting sounds. However, in my original instrument proposal, some sounds are not triggered immediately but only when the falling icons reach the ground. Although this delay reduces direct control, it may create a more natural connection between the visual animation and the sound.

Through this experiment, I want to explore whether users prefer direct control over the music and rhythm, or are comfortable with some delayed, random and emergent feedback, allowing sounds to occur naturally through visual events. This will help me find an appropriate balance between direct control and open-ended exploration.

Prototype 1: drum by click

In the first prototype, I use a simple click interaction to directly trigger the drum sound, using it as a baseline for the mapping experiment. This version turns the instrument into a common “click-to-sound” interaction with a different visual layout. Each user action receives immediate auditory feedback, making the mapping between interaction and sound direct and easy to understand. I designed this prototype as a comparison with later versions that use delayed feedback and more indirect methods of triggering sound.

Prototype 2: drum and sound triggered together

In the second prototype, I experimented with triggering both the musical sound and drum sound only when the falling icon reaches the ground. Unlike the immediate feedback in the first prototype, users first see the falling animation, while the sound is produced when the icon lands. The main purpose of this prototype is to explore how users respond to delayed feedback and whether this less immediate mapping is still easy to understand. I also want to test how triggering the drum and musical sound together on landing affects the overall musical and playing experience.

Prototype 3: sound triggered immediately while drum is delayed

In the third prototype, the musical sound and drum sound are mapped to different stages of the interaction. The musical sound is triggered immediately when the user clicks, while the drum sound is triggered only when the emoji reaches the ground. This prototype combines both immediate and delayed feedback. Through this prototype, I want to test whether combining these two types of mapping is still easy for users to understand, and how separating the musical sound and percussion in time affects the overall playing experience. I also want to explore how users respond to this form of partial control, where they can directly control the musical sound while the percussion is determined by the falling animation.
